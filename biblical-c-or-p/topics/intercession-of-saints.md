# Intercession of the Saints

## The question

May believers on earth ask departed saints (and Mary) to pray for them, on the premise that the saints in heaven are alive, aware, and able to intercede — or does scripture restrict prayer and requests for intercession to God alone, with the living as the only fellow-intercessors in view?

## Catholic position

- **Hebrews 12:1** — believers are described as surrounded by "so great a cloud of witnesses," read as implying the departed faithful remain aware of and connected to the Church on earth, not disconnected from it.
- **Revelation 5:8** — the twenty-four elders in heaven hold "golden vials full of odours, which are the prayers of saints," depicting heavenly figures actively presenting the prayers of believers on earth to God.
- **Revelation 6:9–10** — martyred souls under the altar cry out to God on behalf of justice on earth, read as evidence the departed remain engaged in intercessory prayer.
- **Luke 16:19–31** (Lazarus and the rich man) — the rich man, though damned, intercedes (unsuccessfully) for his living brothers, read as showing the dead retain awareness of and concern for the living; if even the damned retain this awareness, the argument runs, the glorified saints surely do as well.
- **Matthew 17:1–3** (the Transfiguration) — Moses and Elijah, long dead, appear conversing with Jesus, read as evidence the departed remain active and can be present/engaged in the affairs of the living.
- Catholic theology frames asking a saint to pray as no different in kind from asking a living Christian friend to pray for you — an appeal to the doctrine of the Communion of Saints, in which death does not sever the Church's unity in Christ.

Primary source: *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §946–962 ("The Communion of Saints"); §2683 (specifically on the intercession of saints).

## Protestant position

- **1 Timothy 2:5** — "there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus," cited as establishing Christ as the sole mediator/intercessor to whom believers should turn, without additional intermediaries.
- **Hebrews 4:14–16** — believers are told to "come boldly unto the throne of grace" directly, because Christ himself, the great high priest, sympathizes with human weakness — read as removing any need for a saintly intermediary to gain access to God.
- **Hebrews 7:25** — Christ "ever liveth to make intercession" for believers, read as Christ's own heavenly intercession being sufficient, with no scriptural instruction to also petition departed saints.
- No New Testament text records anyone praying *to* a deceased believer or *asking* a deceased believer to intercede; the biblical pattern throughout Acts and the epistles is believers asking *living* fellow Christians for prayer (e.g. **Romans 15:30**, **Ephesians 6:19**, **1 Thessalonians 5:25**, **James 5:16**).
- **Isaiah 8:19** — "should not a people seek unto their God? for the living to the dead?" is read (in context, about necromancy/consulting mediums) by some Protestants as a broader caution against seeking counsel or communication from the dead rather than from God.
- **Deuteronomy 18:10–11** forbids consulting the dead ("a necromancer") as part of a broader prohibition on divination; while Catholics distinguish this sharply from asking a saint's intercession (not consulting the dead for hidden knowledge, but requesting prayer, analogous to Hebrews 12:1's "cloud of witnesses"), Protestants generally read the prohibition as counseling caution about any direct communication addressed to the dead.

Primary sources: *Westminster Confession of Faith* XXI.2–3 (prayer to be made to God alone); *Thirty-Nine Articles* Article XXII (rejecting "Invocation of Saints" alongside Purgatory).

## Common ground

Both traditions affirm the departed faithful are alive with Christ (not annihilated or unconscious), affirm the "communion of saints" as a real spiritual unity of believers across death (a phrase both traditions confess in the Apostles' Creed), and affirm intercessory prayer among believers is commanded and valuable. The dispute is specifically about whether that communion licenses direct requests addressed to departed saints, or whether it remains true but without a scriptural warrant for that specific practice.

- Job 19:25–27 (Job's hope of seeing his Redeemer after death)
- Luke 20:37–38 ("he is not a God of the dead, but of the living")
