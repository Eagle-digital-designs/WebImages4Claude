# The Sacraments

## The question

Did Christ institute seven sacraments as ordinary channels of grace, or only two ordinances (baptism and the Lord's Supper) that were directly and explicitly commanded by Christ himself as sign-acts for the whole Church?

## Catholic position

Catholic theology holds Christ instituted seven sacraments, each defended from a distinct set of texts:

- **Baptism** — Matthew 28:19; John 3:5 (see [`baptism.md`](baptism.md))
- **Confirmation** — Acts 8:14–17 and Acts 19:1–6, where the Holy Spirit is given through the laying on of apostolic hands in a distinct act from baptism itself
- **Eucharist** — Matthew 26:26–28; John 6:53–58 (see [`the-eucharist.md`](the-eucharist.md))
- **Penance/Reconciliation** — John 20:22–23 (see [`confession-and-absolution.md`](confession-and-absolution.md))
- **Anointing of the Sick** — James 5:14–15, "Is any sick among you? let him call for the elders of the church; and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord: And the prayer of faith shall save the sick"
- **Holy Orders** — 1 Timothy 4:14 ("the gift... given thee... with the laying on of the hands of the presbytery") and 2 Timothy 1:6, read as evidence of a specific ordaining rite conferring ministerial grace and authority
- **Matrimony** — Ephesians 5:31–32, where marriage is directly called "a great mystery" (Greek *mysterion*, Latin *sacramentum*) in reference to Christ and the Church

Catholic theology defines a sacrament as an outward sign, instituted by Christ, that confers the grace it signifies — a definition all seven are held to meet, drawing on both direct scripture and the Church's authoritative discernment of apostolic practice (see [`scripture-and-tradition.md`](scripture-and-tradition.md)).

Primary source: *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §1113–1134; Council of Trent, *Decree on the Sacraments* (1547).

## Protestant position

Protestant traditions generally recognize only two ordinances as instituted by Christ himself for the whole Church:

- **Baptism** — directly commanded by Christ in Matthew 28:19 ("baptizing them")
- **The Lord's Supper** — directly commanded by Christ himself at the Last Supper, with an explicit repetition instruction ("this do in remembrance of me," Luke 22:19; 1 Corinthians 11:24–25)

The Protestant argument is that only these two meet the criterion of a visible sign *directly instituted by Christ in his own earthly ministry, commanded for the whole Church, with a specific repeated action*:

- On Confirmation, Acts 8 and 19 are read as historically descriptive of a specific transitional moment in the earliest, Spirit-filled church rather than establishing a repeatable sacrament for all believers going forward.
- On Anointing the Sick, James 5 is affirmed by most Protestants as a valid, biblical practice of prayer for healing (many continue it), but is not typically classified as a "sacrament" carrying sacramental grace in the Catholic sense — the emphasis in the text is placed on "the prayer of faith," not the oil itself.
- On Holy Orders, the laying on of hands in 1 and 2 Timothy is read as commissioning for a specific ministry role (recognizing and setting apart gifting already given), not conferring a permanent, ontological priestly character distinct from the "priesthood of all believers" (1 Peter 2:9, Revelation 1:6).
- On Matrimony, Protestants affirm marriage is a profound, God-ordained "mystery" reflecting Christ and the Church (Ephesians 5), but distinguish this theological significance from sacramental status, since marriage predates Christ's institution and is not unique to the Church (unbelievers marry validly too).

Primary sources: *Westminster Confession of Faith* XXVII–XXIX (only Baptism and the Lord's Supper named as sacraments); Martin Luther, *The Babylonian Captivity of the Church* (1520), which narrowed the medieval seven to two (occasionally three, briefly including Penance) on the criterion of direct dominical institution.

## Common ground

Both traditions affirm baptism and the Lord's Supper as the two most central, universally observed rites, both instituted directly by Christ. Both affirm the underlying practices behind the other five Catholic sacraments are biblical and valuable (confirmation of the Spirit's work, confession of sin, prayer for the sick, ordination to ministry, the sanctity of marriage) — the dispute is specifically about whether those five carry sacramental status (an instituted, grace-conferring sign) or are important but non-sacramental practices.

## Search terms

- 1 Peter 2:9 ("a royal priesthood") and Revelation 1:6, 5:10 (priesthood of all believers)
- Numbers 8:10 and Deuteronomy 34:9 (Old Testament laying on of hands for commissioning)
- Genesis 2:24 (the original institution of marriage, prior to the Church)
- Mark 6:13 (the twelve anointing the sick with oil during Jesus' earthly ministry)
- Acts 6:6 (laying on of hands for the appointment of the first deacons)
