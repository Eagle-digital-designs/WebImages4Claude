# The Eucharist

## The question

When Jesus said "this is my body," did he mean it literally, such that the consecrated bread and wine truly become his body and blood (while retaining the appearance of bread and wine) — or did he speak symbolically, instituting a memorial meal that represents his body and blood?

## Catholic position

- **Matthew 26:26–28, Mark 14:22–24, Luke 22:19–20** — at the Last Supper, Jesus says of the bread "Take, eat; this is my body," and of the cup "this is my blood of the new testament." Read at face value, without an explicit qualifier ("this represents" or "this symbolizes") in any account.
- **John 6:51–58** — "the bread that I will give is my flesh... Except ye eat the flesh of the Son of man, and drink his blood, ye have no life in you... For my flesh is meat indeed, and my blood is drink indeed." Read as Jesus intensifying rather than softening the literal claim in response to the crowd's shock (John 6:52, 60), rather than clarifying it as merely figurative.
- **1 Corinthians 11:27–29** — Paul warns that eating the bread or drinking the cup of the Lord "unworthily" makes one "guilty of the body and blood of the Lord," and that failing to "discern the Lord's body" brings judgment — read as implying a real presence serious enough to carry that weight of consequence.
- **1 Corinthians 10:16** — "The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not the communion of the blood of Christ? The bread which we break, is it not the communion of the body of Christ?"
- Catholic theology (formalized as transubstantiation) holds the substance of the bread and wine is changed into the substance of Christ's body and blood while the appearances (accidents) remain bread and wine.

Primary source: *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §1373–1381; Council of Trent, *Decree on the Holy Eucharist* (1551).

## Protestant position

- Protestant traditions diverge among themselves here (Lutheran "sacramental union," Reformed "spiritual real presence," and Baptist/Zwinglian "memorial" views are not identical), but broadly read the Last Supper accounts against Jesus' frequent use of "is" for figurative identification elsewhere: **John 10:9** ("I am the door"), **John 15:1** ("I am the true vine"), **John 6:35** ("I am the bread of life") — none taken as literal physical transformation claims.
- **Luke 22:19** — "this do in remembrance of me" — read as framing the meal explicitly as a memorial act.
- **1 Corinthians 11:24–26** — Paul repeats "in remembrance of me" and states the meal proclaims ("shew") the Lord's death "till he come," language read as pointing back to a past, completed, once-for-all event rather than a repeated re-presentation.
- **Hebrews 9:25–28 and 10:10–14** — Christ's sacrifice is described as occurring "once" and never to be repeated ("once in the end of the world hath he appeared to put away sin," "we are sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all"), read by many Protestants as in tension with any sacrificial re-presentation of Christ in the Mass.
- On John 6, the Reformed/memorialist reading notes Jesus explicitly glosses his own hard sayings as spiritual rather than literal just a few verses later: **John 6:63** — "It is the spirit that quickeneth; the flesh profiteth nothing: the words that I speak unto you, they are spirit, and they are life."

Primary sources: *Westminster Confession of Faith* XXIX ("Of the Lord's Supper"); Huldrych Zwingli's memorial view; Martin Luther's *Small Catechism* (Lutherans affirm a real, though not transubstantiated, sacramental presence — closer to the Catholic position than other Protestant views on this specific point).

## Common ground

All traditions agree the Lord's Supper was instituted by Christ himself, is to be observed by the Church, involves bread and wine, and is not a merely empty or optional ritual — it communicates something real about union with Christ and his sacrifice. Lutheran and Reformed traditions in particular affirm more of a "real presence" than the memorial view, so this is not simply "Catholic vs. Protestant" as a clean binary — the range of Protestant positions itself spans from close to the Catholic view (Lutheran) to fully symbolic (Zwinglian/Baptist).
