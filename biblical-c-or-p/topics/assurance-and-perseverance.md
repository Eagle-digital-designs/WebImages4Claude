# Assurance and Perseverance

## The question

Once a person is truly saved, is their salvation eternally secure and guaranteed to persevere to the end — or can a believer, through serious (mortal) sin or apostasy, forfeit the grace of salvation they once genuinely had?

*Note: this dispute does not split cleanly along Catholic/Protestant lines — Reformed/Calvinist Protestants hold "perseverance of the saints" (eternal security), while Arminian/Wesleyan/Pentecostal Protestants hold salvation can be lost, a position closer to the Catholic view on this specific point. It is included here because it is a live and frequently-raised point of difference between Catholic teaching and much of mainstream evangelical Protestantism.*

## Catholic position

- **1 Corinthians 9:27** — Paul says he disciplines his own body, "lest that by any means, when I have preached to others, I myself should be a castaway," read as the apostle himself acknowledging the real possibility of disqualification even after extensive ministry.
- **Hebrews 6:4–6** — describes those "once enlightened," who "tasted of the heavenly gift," "were made partakers of the Holy Ghost," and yet "fall away," for whom renewal to repentance is said to be impossible — read as describing genuine believers who can and do fall away entirely.
- **Hebrews 10:26–29** — willful, ongoing sin after receiving "the knowledge of the truth" is said to leave "no more sacrifice for sins," and one who does so is said to have "trodden under foot the Son of God" and treated "the blood of the covenant... an unholy thing."
- **Ezekiel 18:24** — "when the righteous turneth away from his righteousness, and committeth iniquity... all his righteousness that he hath done shall not be remembered... in his sin that he hath sinned... shall he die," read as an Old Testament principle carried into New Testament warnings.
- **1 John 5:16–17** — distinguishes "a sin unto death" from sin "not unto death," which Catholic theology maps onto its distinction between mortal sin (which severs the state of grace) and venial sin (which wounds but does not destroy it).
- **John 15:1–6** — branches "in me" (already in union with Christ) that fail to bear fruit are cut off and burned, read as describing genuinely grafted-in believers who can be removed.
- Catholic theology holds a believer in a state of grace can, through a freely chosen, fully-consented-to grave (mortal) sin, lose that grace entirely and must be restored through repentance and confession (see [`confession-and-absolution.md`](confession-and-absolution.md)).

Primary source: *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §1854–1864 (mortal and venial sin); Council of Trent, *Decree on Justification*, Canons 23 and 27.

## Protestant position (Reformed / eternal security view)

- **John 10:27–29** — "My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me: And I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand," read as an unconditional guarantee grounded in Christ's own power to keep, not the believer's own effort.
- **Romans 8:29–30** — the "golden chain" of predestined, called, justified, glorified, presented as a single unbroken sequence with no exit point described.
- **Romans 8:38–39** — "nor things present, nor things to come... shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord," read as excluding even a believer's own future sin from severing that bond.
- **Philippians 1:6** — "he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ," read as God's own faithfulness, not the believer's, being what guarantees the outcome.
- **Ephesians 1:13–14** — believers are "sealed with that holy Spirit of promise, which is the earnest [down payment/guarantee] of our inheritance," read as the Spirit's presence itself functioning as a divine guarantee of final salvation.
- On Hebrews 6, the Reformed reading typically holds the passage describes people who experienced real, close exposure to the Spirit's work and the community of faith (illumination, tasting) without ever possessing genuine saving faith themselves — the sobering warning is read as a real warning against false, temporary faith, not evidence a truly regenerate person can be lost, since 1 John 2:19 states of apostates: "they went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would no doubt have continued with us."

## Protestant position (Arminian / Wesleyan / conditional security view)

- Affirms the same warning passages (Hebrews 6, Hebrews 10, John 15) more directly, as genuinely describing believers who can and sometimes do fall away through unbelief or sin, while still holding to salvation by grace through faith alone (not by works, distinguishing this from the Catholic mortal-sin framework).
- **2 Peter 2:20–22** — those who "have escaped the pollutions of the world through the knowledge of the Lord and Saviour" and are again entangled and overcome are said to be "worse" off than before, read as describing a real, if tragic, possibility of falling from a genuinely escaped state.
- **Galatians 5:4** — "Christ is become of no effect unto you, whosoever of you are justified by the law; ye are fallen from grace," read as describing believers (Paul's actual audience) who had "fallen from grace."

Primary sources: *Westminster Confession of Faith* XVII ("Of the Perseverance of the Saints," the classic Reformed statement); the Arminian *Five Articles of Remonstrance* (1610), Article V; Wesley's sermons on Christian perfection and the possibility of apostasy.

## Common ground

All positions affirm salvation is a gift of grace, not human achievement; affirm that God is faithful and does not abandon those who remain in genuine faith; and affirm that a person who commits apostasy or persists unrepentant in serious sin is in real spiritual danger. The dispute is specifically about whether that danger can ever actually be realized in someone who was truly, savingly regenerate, or whether apparent falling-away always indicates the faith was never genuine (or, on the Catholic side, that grace once given can genuinely be forfeited and later restored).
