# Confession and Absolution

## The question

Must sin committed after baptism be confessed to a priest, who pronounces absolution by delegated authority — or does scripture teach believers confess sin directly to God, with no priestly intermediary required for forgiveness?

## Catholic position

- **John 20:21–23** — the risen Christ breathes on the apostles and says, "Receive ye the Holy Ghost: Whose soever sins ye remit, they are remitted unto them; and whose soever sins ye retain, they are retained." Read as a direct grant of authority to forgive or withhold forgiveness of specific sins — an authority that requires knowing what the sin is, which in turn implies a mechanism (confession) for that knowledge to reach the one exercising the authority.
- **Matthew 16:19 and 18:18** — the binding/loosing authority given to Peter and the apostles is read as including authority over the forgiveness of sins, exercised on Christ's behalf.
- **James 5:14–16** — "Is any sick among you? let him call for the elders of the church... Confess your faults one to another... The prayer of faith shall save the sick... and if he have committed sins, they shall be forgiven him," read as instituting a practice of confession involving the Church's elders, connected to healing and forgiveness.
- **John 1:9** and Catholic sacramental theology hold that Christ instituted specific, visible means (sacraments) to communicate invisible grace reliably, rather than leaving forgiveness solely to private, unwitnessed transactions between the individual and God — Confession (Reconciliation) is one of the seven sacraments on this reasoning.
- Acts 19:18 — new believers at Ephesus "confessed, and shewed their deeds," an early example of public/communal confession of sin within the believing community.

Primary source: *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §1440–1470 ("The Sacrament of Penance and Reconciliation").

## Protestant position

- **1 John 1:9** — "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness," addressed to "we" (believers) confessing directly to God ("he"), with no priestly intermediary named.
- **Psalm 32:5** and **Psalm 51** — David's model of confession is direct address to God ("I acknowledged my sin unto thee... I said, I will confess my transgressions unto the LORD; and thou forgavest the iniquity of my sin").
- **Hebrews 4:14–16** — believers are invited to "come boldly unto the throne of grace" directly for mercy, through the great high priest Christ, without a further human priestly mediator for absolution.
- **1 Timothy 2:5** — again cited (as in the papacy and Marian topics) for the principle of Christ as sole mediator between God and humanity.
- On John 20:23, the Protestant reading (especially Reformed and Baptist) holds this authority was either unique to the apostles as founders of the Church (declarative — the apostles authoritatively *announced* whose sins were forgiven based on their response to the gospel, rather than performing a repeatable sacramental transaction), or is exercised corporately by the church body today only in a declarative sense (e.g. through church discipline, per Matthew 18:15–17), not through private, individual priestly absolution.
- On James 5, many Protestants read "confess your faults one to another" as mutual, non-hierarchical confession among believers generally (consistent with "one to another," a mutual/reciprocal phrase), not confession specifically and exclusively to an ordained priest.

Primary sources: *Westminster Confession of Faith* XV (repentance unto life, addressed directly to God); Martin Luther retained private confession as spiritually valuable but rejected it as a sacrament required for salvation, a position distinct from later Reformed and Baptist traditions that dropped the practice of formal confession to a minister altogether.

## Common ground

Both traditions affirm sin must be confessed and repented of, affirm confession (private or corporate) has real spiritual value within the church community, and affirm forgiveness ultimately comes from God through Christ's atonement, not from any human authority acting independently of that atonement. The dispute is specifically about whether a priest's role in confession is a necessary, divinely instituted means through which that forgiveness is normally channeled, or a pastoral practice without sacramental necessity.

## Search terms

- Leviticus 5:5–6 (Old Testament confession connected to a priestly sin offering)
- 2 Samuel 12:13 (Nathan's direct pronouncement to David: "The LORD also hath put away thy sin")
- Matthew 18:15–20 (church discipline process, "tell it unto the church")
- Luke 15:11–24 (the Prodigal Son's direct confession to the father, with no intermediary in the parable)
- Proverbs 28:13 (confessing and forsaking sin brings mercy)
