# Images and Veneration

## The question

Does the use of statues and icons of Christ, Mary, and the saints in worship spaces — and the practice of bowing before or kissing them — violate the biblical prohibition on graven images, or is it a legitimate form of honor (veneration) that is categorically distinct from the idolatrous worship (adoration) the commandment forbids?

## Catholic position

- **Exodus 20:4–5** itself is read in context alongside **Exodus 25:18–20** and **1 Kings 6:23–29**, where God commands Moses and Solomon to make graven images of cherubim for the tabernacle and temple — read as showing the prohibition targets making images *to worship as gods* (idolatry), not the making of religious images as such, since God commands both in the same broader narrative.
- **Numbers 21:8–9** — God commands Moses to make a bronze serpent and lift it up, such that anyone bitten who looked at it would live — a divinely commanded image used as a physical instrument of God's saving action, later referenced approvingly by Jesus himself as a type of the crucifixion (John 3:14–15).
- Catholic theology draws a firm doctrinal distinction between **latria** (adoration, due to God alone), **dulia** (honor/veneration, given to saints), and **hyperdulia** (a higher veneration given uniquely to Mary) — honoring an image of Christ or a saint is understood as honoring the person represented, not worshiping the physical object itself, comparable to honoring a photograph of a loved one.
- **Exodus 20:4** forbids images "to bow down thyself to them, and serve them" — Catholic theology reads the operative prohibition as directed at *serving/worshiping* the image as a deity, which veneration (bowing in honor, without worship) is held not to constitute.
- This question was formally addressed by the **Second Council of Nicaea (787 AD)**, which affirmed that the honor given to an image passes to its prototype (the person depicted), against the iconoclast controversy of the 8th century.

Primary source: *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §2129–2132; Second Council of Nicaea (787 AD).

## Protestant position

- **Exodus 20:4–6** — "Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image, or any likeness of any thing... Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them, nor serve them," read by many Protestants (especially Reformed traditions) as forbidding both the making of such images for religious use *and* any bowing before them, without the latria/dulia distinction being read into the text itself.
- **Deuteronomy 4:15–19** — Moses recalls that Israel "saw no manner of similitude" of God at Sinai, warning against making "the similitude of any figure" specifically *because* no form was seen — read as grounding the prohibition in God's own invisibility/transcendence, a concern that would apply to any image purporting to represent a divine person, not solely to images formally worshiped as gods.
- **Romans 1:22–23** — Paul describes the exchange of God's glory for "an image made like to corruptible man" as a mark of humanity's fall into idolatry, cited as a caution about the spiritual danger of physical representations in worship regardless of stated intent.
- **Isaiah 44:9–20** — a lengthy denunciation of idol-making, mocking a craftsman who uses part of a log for fuel and part to fashion "a god" to bow down to — Protestants note the text does not exempt images made with a claimed intention of mere honor rather than worship.
- On the bronze serpent (Numbers 21), Protestants point to **2 Kings 18:4**, where King Hezekiah, generations later, destroys that same bronze serpent (calling it "Nehushtan") because Israel "did burn incense to it," read as scripture's own verdict that even a divinely-commanded image became an occasion of idolatry over time and was rightly destroyed — used as a caution about the practical difficulty of maintaining the veneration/worship distinction in practice.
- Reformed traditions in particular (following Calvin) historically removed images and statues from worship spaces on this reasoning, distinguishing themselves from Lutheran and Anglican traditions, which retained more religious art while still typically rejecting the invocation or veneration of the images themselves.

Primary sources: *Westminster Larger Catechism* Q109 (duties forbidden in the Second Commandment, explicitly listing "the making of any representation of God" and "all worshipping of it, or God in it"); John Calvin, *Institutes* I.11.

## Common ground

Both traditions affirm the first and second commandments forbid idolatry — worshiping any created thing, image, or representation as though it were God. Both affirm God alone is to receive worship (latria). Neither tradition believes a wooden or painted object has any inherent power. The dispute is specifically about whether the commandment's scope extends to prohibiting the making and honoring of religious images altogether, or is limited to images made and treated as objects of worship in themselves.

## Search terms

- Exodus 25:18–22 (the cherubim over the mercy seat, in the same book as the prohibition)
- 2 Chronicles 3:10–13 (Solomon's temple cherubim)
- 1 Corinthians 8:4–6 ("an idol is nothing in the world," reframing the issue around the object's spiritual reality rather than its physical form alone)
- Acts 17:29 (Paul at Athens arguing the Godhead should not be thought "like unto gold, or silver, or stone")
- Genesis 1:26–27 (mankind itself made "in the image of God" — a different Hebrew usage of "image" worth comparing)
