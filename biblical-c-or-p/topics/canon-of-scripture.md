# Canon of Scripture

## The question

Why do Catholic and Protestant Old Testaments differ, and which set of books is the correct canon of scripture?

## The actual disagreement, precisely stated

The New Testament canon is **not** in dispute — Catholics and Protestants agree on the same 27 New Testament books. The disagreement is entirely about the Old Testament: Catholic Bibles include seven additional books (and additions to two others) that Protestant Bibles exclude, commonly called the **Deuterocanonical books** (Catholic terminology) or the **Apocrypha** (Protestant terminology):

- Tobit
- Judith
- 1 and 2 Maccabees
- Wisdom (of Solomon)
- Sirach (Ecclesiasticus)
- Baruch
- Additions to Esther and Daniel (including the stories of Susanna and Bel and the Dragon)

## Catholic position

- These books appear in the **Septuagint** (LXX), the Greek translation of the Old Testament that was the Bible most widely used and quoted by the apostles and the early Church — most New Testament Old Testament quotations align more closely with the Septuagint's wording than the Hebrew Masoretic Text.
- The books were treated as scripture by many early Church Fathers and were read in Christian liturgy from an early date.
- They were formally, authoritatively defined as canonical scripture at the **Council of Rome (382 AD)** under Pope Damasus I, and again at the regional **Councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397, 419)**, and definitively at the **Council of Trent (1546)** in response to their exclusion by the Reformers.
- **2 Timothy 3:16** ("all scripture is given by inspiration of God") does not itself list which books count as scripture — Catholic theology holds the question of *which* books are inspired is a matter the Church's teaching authority settled, not one scripture answers for itself (compare [`scripture-and-tradition.md`](scripture-and-tradition.md)).
- Some New Testament passages appear to allude to or echo language found in these books (e.g., Hebrews 11:35's mention of those "tortured, not accepting deliverance" is often connected to 2 Maccabees 7), read as evidence of their early acceptance and influence.

## Protestant position

- These books were **not part of the Hebrew canon** used by Jews in Palestine, and Jewish tradition (affirmed by the 1st-century historian Josephus, and reflected in later rabbinic sources) recognized a closed set of 22 or 24 books corresponding to the 39 books of the Protestant Old Testament.
- **Jesus and the New Testament authors never quote any of the Deuterocanonical books as scripture**, despite quoting the rest of the Old Testament extensively and often introducing quotations with formulas like "it is written." (This is an argument from the absence of citation, and is disputed in force but not in the underlying fact.)
- The 4th-century biblical scholar **Jerome**, commissioned to produce the Latin Vulgate, distinguished these books as *libri ecclesiastici* — useful for edification but not for establishing doctrine — placing them in a separate category from the *libri canonici*, even while including them in his translation; Reformers pointed to this ancient internal distinction as precedent.
- The books themselves contain internal indications some Protestants read as evidence against their inspired status — e.g., **1 Maccabees 9:27** states that prophecy had ceased in Israel by that time ("there was not a prophet seen"), and the prologue to Sirach, written by the translator (the author's grandson), explicitly describes the work as a human composition being translated, not a claim of direct divine inspiration.
- The Reformers (Luther, and the framers of the Protestant confessions) removed these books from the Old Testament proper, printing them in a separate "Apocrypha" section (if at all) explicitly marked as non-canonical but potentially useful reading, rather than scripture for establishing doctrine.

Primary sources: *Westminster Confession of Faith* I.3 ("The books commonly called Apocrypha... are no part of the canon of Scripture... and therefore are of no authority in the Church of God"); *Thirty-Nine Articles* Article VI.

## Common ground

Both traditions agree on the entire New Testament canon (27 books) and on the 39 books that make up the Protestant Old Testament / the "protocanonical" books within the Catholic Old Testament. The size of the actual disagreement is smaller than it can appear — seven additional books plus some textual additions, out of a shared library of dozens of books both traditions already agree on completely.

## Search terms

- Luke 24:44 (Jesus' own threefold division of the Hebrew scriptures: "the law of Moses, and in the prophets, and in the psalms")
- Matthew 23:35 (Jesus' reference to Abel-to-Zechariah, read by some as bracketing the full extent of the Hebrew canon as it was ordered in his day)
- Romans 3:2 (the Jews "were committed the oracles of God")
- Hebrews 11 (the "hall of faith," and its possible echoes of 2 Maccabees 7 in verse 35)
- The Septuagint (LXX) and Masoretic Text as two textual traditions worth comparing directly for a given Old Testament passage
