# Baptism

## The question

Does baptism itself effect regeneration (new spiritual birth), such that infants of believing families should be baptized as an entry into the covenant community — or is baptism strictly a believer's public profession of a faith and regeneration that has already occurred, reserved for those old enough to make that profession?

## Catholic position

- **John 3:5** — "Except a man be born of water and of the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God," read as tying regeneration directly to a water rite.
- **Acts 2:38–39** — Peter says "Repent, and be baptized every one of you... for the remission of sins," and adds "the promise is unto you, and to your children," read as extending baptism's benefit explicitly to children/infants of believers.
- **Acts 16:15, 16:33, and 1 Corinthians 1:16** — household baptisms (Lydia's household, the Philippian jailer's household, the household of Stephanas), read as very likely including infants or young children present in a first-century household, consistent with a longstanding covenantal practice of including the whole family.
- **Colossians 2:11–12** — baptism is directly likened to circumcision ("the circumcision of Christ"), the Old Testament covenant sign that was administered to infant males on the eighth day (Genesis 17:12), read as implying baptism inherits that covenantal, infant-inclusive pattern in the New Testament.
- **Titus 3:5** — "he saved us, by the washing of regeneration," read as directly connecting a washing (baptism) to the act of regeneration itself.
- **1 Peter 3:21** — "baptism doth also now save us... the answer of a good conscience toward God," read as a direct scriptural statement that baptism saves.
- Catholic theology holds baptism (for infants and adults alike) is the ordinary instrumental means by which original sin is washed away and regenerating grace is first given, consistent with Christ's own command to "baptize all nations" (Matthew 28:19) without an age restriction stated.

Primary source: *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §1213–1284.

## Protestant position

- **Acts 2:38** itself is read by many Protestants (particularly Baptist traditions) as sequencing repentance before baptism, and the pattern throughout Acts consistently shows baptism following a personal profession of faith (Acts 8:12, 8:36–38 "if thou believest with all thine heart, thou mayest," 16:31–33 "believe on the Lord Jesus Christ... and was baptized").
- **Mark 16:16** — "He that believeth and is baptized shall be saved," read as ordering belief before baptism as a normal pattern.
- **Romans 6:3–4** — baptism symbolizes union with Christ's death and resurrection, requiring the conscious faith the passage assumes the baptized person already has ("we... were baptized into his death... that like as Christ was raised up... even so we also should walk in newness of life").
- On 1 Peter 3:21 specifically, the same verse continues by defining what "saves": "not the putting away of the filth of the flesh, but the answer of a good conscience toward God" — read by many Protestants as clarifying the passage is not describing the physical water rite mechanically saving, but the inward reality (a conscience cleared through faith) that baptism pictures.
- No New Testament narrative explicitly records an infant being baptized upon their own profession of faith (household baptisms do not state infants were present or excluded either way, and this remains an argument from silence on both sides).
- Reformed (paedobaptist) and Baptist (credobaptist) traditions are themselves split on infant baptism: Reformed Protestants (e.g. Presbyterians) retain infant baptism as a covenant sign parallel to circumcision (agreeing with the Catholic argument from Colossians 2 on this specific point, while still rejecting baptismal regeneration itself), while Baptists, Pentecostals, and most evangelical traditions reject infant baptism entirely, requiring a credible profession of faith first.

Primary sources: *Westminster Confession of Faith* XXVIII (Reformed/paedobaptist view — a covenant sign, not regenerative in itself); London Baptist Confession of 1689, Chapter XXIX (credobaptist view, baptism only for professed believers).

## Common ground

All traditions affirm baptism was commanded by Christ (Matthew 28:19), is the normal entry rite into the visible Church, and is not merely optional. Reformed Protestants and Catholics both practice infant baptism, though for different theological reasons (covenant sign vs. regenerative washing) — so this dispute does not divide neatly along a single Catholic/Protestant line; it is at least a three-way conversation between Catholic/regenerative, Reformed/covenantal, and Baptist/credobaptist positions.

## Search terms

- Genesis 17:9–14 (circumcision as the Old Testament covenant sign given to infants)
- Luke 18:15–17 (Jesus welcoming infants — "of such is the kingdom of God" — read on both sides)
- Ezekiel 36:25–27 (water and Spirit together in an Old Testament promise of renewal)
- Acts 10:44–48 (Cornelius's household receives the Spirit before being baptized, a sequence some cite as evidence against baptismal regeneration)
- 1 Corinthians 7:14 (children of even one believing parent described as "holy," cited in the paedobaptist argument)
