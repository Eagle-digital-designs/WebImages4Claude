# Purgatory

## The question

Is there an intermediate state after death in which souls destined for heaven, but not yet fully purified of the temporal effects of sin, undergo purification before entering God's presence — or does scripture teach that a believer passes immediately and completely into Christ's presence at death, with no further purification needed or possible?

## Catholic position

- **2 Maccabees 12:39–45** — Judas Maccabeus takes up a collection to offer atoning sacrifice for fallen soldiers "that they might be delivered from sin," described as "a holy and wholesome thought to pray for the dead." (This book is part of the Catholic Old Testament canon but not the Protestant one — see [`canon-of-scripture.md`](canon-of-scripture.md).)
- **1 Corinthians 3:11–15** — Paul describes each person's work being tested "by fire," where some work is "burned" and the person "suffer[s] loss: but he himself shall be saved; yet so as by fire." Read as describing a purifying process after death for those who are ultimately saved.
- **Matthew 12:32** — Jesus says a certain sin "shall not be forgiven him, neither in this world, neither in the world to come," read by some Catholic interpreters as implying at least the conceptual possibility of some sins being addressed in the world to come.
- **2 Timothy 1:16–18** — Paul prays for mercy for Onesiphorus "in that day" (of judgment), and the household is greeted in a way some read as implying Onesiphorus had already died — cited as an example of prayer for the dead within scripture itself.
- **Revelation 21:27** — "there shall in no wise enter into it [the New Jerusalem] any thing that defileth," read as implying some process ensures nothing impure enters heaven, consistent with a purification after death for those who die imperfectly sanctified.
- Catholic theology holds purgatory is not a "second chance" for the unsaved or a place of doubt about final salvation — it applies only to those already assured of heaven, purifying what remains of sin's temporal effects (not eternal punishment, which Christ has already fully born).

Primary source: *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §1030–1032; Council of Trent, *Decree on Purgatory* (1563).

## Protestant position

- Protestants reject 2 Maccabees as scripture (it is part of the Apocrypha, not the Hebrew canon Jesus and the apostles used as their Old Testament), so do not accept it as establishing a doctrine.
- **2 Corinthians 5:8** — "we are confident... willing rather to be absent from the body, and to be present with the Lord," read as teaching immediate presence with Christ at death, with no intervening purification.
- **Philippians 1:23** — Paul says to depart (die) is "to be with Christ; which is far better," again read as immediate, not delayed, entry into Christ's presence.
- **Luke 23:43** — Jesus tells the repentant thief on the cross, "Today shalt thou be with me in paradise," read as immediate entry into blessedness with no purgatorial interval, despite the thief having no opportunity for a lifetime of purification or works.
- **Hebrews 9:27** — "it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment," read as allowing no intermediate purifying state between death and final judgment.
- **Hebrews 10:14** — "For by one offering he hath perfected for ever them that are sanctified," read as teaching Christ's sacrifice fully and finally accomplishes the purification of believers, leaving nothing further to be purified after death.
- On 1 Corinthians 3, the Protestant reading holds Paul is describing the testing of a believer's *works and ministry* (what was built on the foundation of Christ) at judgment, not the purification of the believer's *soul* from sin — the person is explicitly said to be saved regardless of the outcome of that testing.

Primary sources: *Westminster Confession of Faith* XXXII.1 (souls of the righteous "immediately" pass into glory); *Thirty-Nine Articles* Article XXII (rejecting "the Romish doctrine concerning Purgatory").

## Common ground

Both traditions agree there is a real distinction between salvation itself (a settled matter through Christ) and the ongoing sanctification of a believer, agree Christ's sacrifice is the sole ground of forgiveness, and agree final judgment is real and consequential. The dispute is specifically about whether any part of sanctification's completion occurs after death and before final glorification, and about the canonical status of the text (2 Maccabees) most directly cited for the doctrine.

## Search terms

- Malachi 3:2–3 (a refiner's fire purifying, read typologically by both sides in different directions)
- 1 Peter 1:6–7 (faith tested "as gold" through trials — read as this-life sanctification by Protestants)
- Zechariah 13:9 (refining "as silver is refined")
- Luke 16:19–31 (Lazarus and the rich man — the fixed "great gulf" between the dead)
- 1 John 1:7, 9 (the blood of Christ cleansing "from all sin," and confession bringing forgiveness "and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness")
